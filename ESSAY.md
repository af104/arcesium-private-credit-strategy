# Why I Built This Project

*Andres Farrulla, Stony Brook University, November 2026*

At the end of October I applied for a Corporate Strategy Intern role at Arcesium. I'm graduating in December with an Economics degree and an Accounting concentration, and I've sent out a lot of applications this year, and I've even made it to a final round. This time I wanted to do something more than click "apply" and hope.

I read the job posting line by line. The team wanted someone who could analyze markets, size opportunities, build financial models, track competitors and put it all into clear writing for leadership. Then there was a line I hadn't seen on many internship postings: the ability to build agent workflows or AI-built widgets. Most applicants would just write "AI tools" in their skills section. I figured the better move was to show it.

## Starting from zero

I'll be honest about where I started. When I picked private credit as the focus, I didn't really understand what it was. I had to learn it in plain terms first. Companies used to borrow mostly from banks. Now a lot of them borrow from investment firms instead. Those firms have to track every loan they hold: who paid, who didn't, how much interest came in, and what to tell their own investors. Arcesium sells the software that does that tracking.

The part that clicked for me was asset-based finance. Instead of one big loan to one company, a firm buys a bundle of thousands of small loans, like car loans or equipment leases. That's a huge amount of data, and a lot of firms still manage it in spreadsheets. As an accounting student, I know how easy it is to make mistakes reconciling a few hundred lines by hand. Thousands of loans every month is a real problem, and that problem is Arcesium's opportunity.

## How I built it

I set up a group of AI research agents, each with its own job. One profiled Arcesium. One sized the private credit market. One covered the big competitors like SS&C, Clearwater and State Street. One covered the smaller specialists and the gaps nobody was filling yet. Their notes came together into one research report.

The most important thing I learned is that AI output is a first draft, not a final answer. I went back and checked every number against its original source. That caught real mistakes. One competitor was described as a loan tool when it actually handles fund documents. A big bank was said to have no private credit product when it actually launched one in 2025. A news item about an Arcesium client win couldn't be found anywhere at first, and only checked out later when the press release went public. If I had sent the memo without checking, any one of those errors could have cost me credibility with someone who knows the industry far better than I do.

From there I wrote a two-page memo with one clear recommendation: Arcesium should lead its private credit push with asset-based finance and position itself as the neutral home for loan-level data. Then I built an Excel model to size it. You can switch between low, base and high scenarios, and every assumption sits in one tab with its source or a label saying it's my estimate.

## What the model taught me

I expected the size of the market to be the big driver. It wasn't. Private credit is around $4 trillion, and even my high case is a small slice of what managers spend on software. What actually moves the answer is how many clients Arcesium wins and how much each one pays. That changed how I think about strategy. A big market isn't the hard part. Winning inside it is.

I also had to be upfront about what I don't know. Nobody publishes how many private credit managers sit in each size bracket or what Arcesium charges. Instead of pretending, I labeled those numbers as estimates and listed them as open questions. I'd rather show my reasoning and be corrected than look certain and be wrong.

## What I'm taking from this

This project made me better at three things I want to keep doing: learning an unfamiliar industry fast, checking my sources instead of trusting the first answer, and turning a pile of research into one clear point someone can act on. It also showed me that AI is most useful when you treat it like a research team you manage, not a machine that does the thinking for you.

Whether or not I get this internship, I now understand a corner of finance I couldn't have explained a month ago. And I have something real to show for it.
